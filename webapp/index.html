A Project Walkthrough

How I found what actually drives greenhouse vegetable prices

From raw public data to a tested, honestly-reported regression model — built as a portfolio project for a Data Analytics Engineer role at Mastronardi Produce.

1
Starting point

Why this project

The question I set out to answer: what actually moves the wholesale price of greenhouse-grown tomatoes, peppers, and cucumbers? Not a guess, not a general industry narrative — an actual, tested, defensible answer, built the way a real analytics engineer would build it: source real data, question every assumption, and report what holds up and what doesn't.

Everything here uses public data, but the process — sourcing, cleaning, joining, modeling, and reporting honestly — mirrors the real work.

2
How the data was collected

Ten sources, twenty-five years, four markets

The final model draws on ten independent public sources, each chosen deliberately rather than defaulted to.

USDA AMS
Wholesale prices, Boston/NY/Chicago/Detroit, 2001–2025
Open-Meteo
Daily weather for the actual growing regions
FRED
Natural gas, currency, CPI, sentiment, diesel, unemployment
Statistics Canada
Ontario greenhouse production & area harvested
Canada federal carbon charge
Verified schedule, 2019 start through 2025 repeal
NOAA / NDMC
California drought severity

Almost none of this came easily on the first try — and that turned out to be the most instructive part of the project.

Silent failure #1 — the USDA price API returned zero rows for peppers, with no error, for an entire session. Peppers have no generic "Peppers" category in USDA's system — only sub-types like "Peppers, Bell Type." Fixed by querying the exact sub-type name.
Silent failure #2 — Bank of Canada's exchange rate series has a real methodology break in 2017 (discontinued "noon rates" replaced by "daily average" rates). Splicing them together would have quietly corrupted 20 years of currency data. Switched to FRED's single continuous series instead.
Slow, not wrong — an early NOAA weather integration worked but took 10–20+ minutes to run. Swapped to Open-Meteo: same data, no API key, full 25-year pull in seconds.
3
How it's organized

A resumable pipeline, joined to one monthly table

Rather than one large script, the project is a numbered sequence of small, single-purpose scripts — one per source, a merge stage, then modeling.

fetch (per source)
→
raw_data/*.csv
→
merge & resample
→
monthly_panel.csv
→
regression

Resumable by design: each fetch script logs exactly which chunk of data it has already pulled. An interrupted run — network drop, closed laptop — can be restarted without re-downloading or duplicating anything. This mattered in practice: the price pull alone eventually produced close to a million rows.

Frequency decision: sources arrive at wildly different granularities — daily prices, daily weather, monthly gas prices, annual production. Everything resamples to a monthly panel: fine enough to preserve real seasonal variation, coarse enough that even StatCan's annual production figures fit in (spread evenly across each year's months, a documented approximation, not a hidden one).

4
How the regression was done

OLS, with two disciplined guardrails

Robust standard errors (HC3)
Commodity price data doesn't have uniform variance across markets and time — HC3 keeps the significance tests trustworthy anyway.
VIF-based filtering
Every candidate variable is checked for overlap with the others before the model is fit. Anything above VIF = 10 is dropped and logged — never silently discarded.
Fixed effects
Month, commodity, and market dummies absorb baseline differences (seasonality, product type, city) without needing to explain them separately.

The VIF guardrail did most of the real work. About a dozen reasonable-sounding variables were tested and excluded — not because they don't matter economically, but because they share the same slow upward trend over 25 years and become statistically indistinguishable once several are in the same model together:

6
What's in it for us

The takeaway

So what

Greenhouse vegetable prices are driven mainly by season and energy cost — winter commands a real premium because field-grown competitors are out of season, and every dollar of heating cost measurably passes through into the price growers charge.

  • Energy cost exposure is quantifiable — supports proactive hedging tied to natural gas price movements.
  • ToBRFV's price impact has been large and sustained — continued investment in disease resistance has a clear economic rationale beyond crop protection alone.
  • Seasonal timing is the dominant lever — production and marketing strategy should keep leaning into the confirmed winter price advantage.
  • Reporting what didn't work — CPI, minimum wage, diesel, unemployment rate — is what makes the model trustworthy, not a weakness in it.
  • With internal company data (procurement costs, SAWP labor permit counts, true monthly production), this model could go from a strong portfolio piece to something genuinely production-grade.
